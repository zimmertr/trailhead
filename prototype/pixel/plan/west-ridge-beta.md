## 2. The Approach and the Start

- **From Prusik Pass.** Walk up to the pass, then traverse around a subsidiary bump on its **north** side. Going over the top of it costs time (2)(5). The path to the base is faint and stays on the north side of the ridge (5). It finishes with a scramble up one more knob (2).
- **From the south.** Source 4 came up from Viviane Lake on a climbers' trail along the south ridge (4). It then had to scramble a short section of the ridge to reach pitch 1 (4).
- **The ground.** A low, rounded hump of jumbled granite blocks. The blocks are blackened with lichen, mottled black-and-white, and spotted lime green and rust orange. Dense dwarf pine (knee-high, bright green, bushy) and bleached dead twigs grow between them, over a little red-brown soil (4, photos 14-16; 5, photo 0277). The **lower ridge** above is a big, slabby, dark grey face of broken steps. A **pale, squared-off pillar** stands on its right skyline, and a few larches grow in cracks on the face (4, photo 17; 1, photo 112015075).
- **Gear stash.** An **overhanging rock** at the base of the route. Parties drop their big packs there and change into rock shoes (2)(5).
- **Balanced rock.** Only the topo names it ("scramble to balanced rock", drawn as a pale boulder on the hump at the foot of the ridge). None of the four readable sources mentions it, and I could not pick it out in any photo.
- **Start tree.** Only the topo names it. Source 2's photo of the start shows small conifer shrubs in the cracks (2, photo 02). Source 1's topo photo shows larches at the left foot of the face (1, 112015075). Neither confirms one particular tree.

So the topo splits the long slab-plus-traverse pitch that (1), (2) and (4) climb in one go into two pitches. (2) says splitting it is reasonable because of the rope drag (2).

### Pitch 1: the White Crack (topo P1)

- **Length and grade.** Long, nearly a full 60 m rope (2). 5.6 (1)(2)(4)(5).
- **Sequence.**
  1. A distinct crack in an **unusually white band of rock**. It is called a wide crack (2) and a hand crack (4), and is around 5.6. It is the "obvious lighter-colored crack" in source 1's photo caption (1)(2)(5).
  2. Blocky climbing up to the crest (1)(2).
  3. An awkward off-width with a flaring lip (2), or an off-width chimney (4). Source 4 notes that a more exposed line exists beside the chimney (4).
  4. It ends on the crest under a **roof or bulge** of stacked, squarish blocks (4)(5).
- **Crux.** Source 2 says the off-width with the flaring lip was the hardest part (2). Nobody calls the pitch exposed. Source 4 soloed it because nothing was committing (4).
- **Falls.** Not described.
- **Belay.** A rappel station with a lot of old slings around a block. It sits on top of a swooping, flared crack, just below the roof (2)(4). Source 4's photo 22 shows a red sling in the crack under the blocks. Three people fit there (2)(4).
- **Side of the crest.** It starts on the face and joins the crest (1). In source 5, one climber went straight up the crest while the other stayed on climber's left (5).
- **Exposure and what lies below.** Moderate. Looking back down you see the lower ridge as a pale jointed rib, then a wide basin of white glacier-polished slabs with larches, and a distant lake (4, photos 19-20; 5, photos 0284 and 0287). One photo of the "more exposed line" shows a sheer pale wall dropping to a talus floor with golden larches (4, photo 23).

### Pitch 2: the Roof, the Trail, the Air Step (topo P2)

- **Length and grade.** Short (topo). Mostly easy, but the roof pull is harder than low fifth class (5). There is no separate grade.
- **Sequence.**
  1. **Stemming corner** between big rounded blocks stained orange and lime with lichen. The climber spreads their legs wide across it (4, photos 25-26; 2).
  2. A **short, burly pull over the roof**, a big overhanging block (2)(4). The leader drops out of sight over it (4).
  3. Traverses and **down-climbs** onto what is clearly a **flat trail** (2). This is the trail on the **north** side (1). Source 4 calls it a short down-climb then an easy traverse of about 8 m (4).
  4. **Climb back up about 15 ft to the south side of the ridge.** Source 1 says the exposure here is great (1). Source 2 calls it one more face section back to the crest (2).
  5. The **"air step"**: a stride across a gap from one boulder to another (2). The topo's "big step" is the same feature (inference). Source 2's photo 03 shows the step at the foot of the slab. It is a large, flat, overhanging tabular block jutting out over the north-side drop.
  6. A short slab to the belay (2).
- **Crux.** The roof pull (2)(4)(5).
- **Falls.** The leader in source 4 placed one piece at the roof before pulling it (4). A slip on the roof would land on that piece (inference). Source 4 used a #0.5, #0.75 and #1 on this pitch (4).
- **Belay.** **Two big horns**, easy to sling (2), a "slingable" block (1), a "slingable horn" (topo). Source 4 instead belayed at a "broad ledge" after the traverse (4). It sits on a broad, flat, speckled platform on the crest, right at the foot of the slab. Three people fit comfortably (4, photo 30; 2, photo 03, where the belayer's feet and the coiled green rope sit on a wide flat slab).
- **Side of the crest.** Over the roof on the crest, then the **north** side on the trail, then back to the **south** side and the crest (1)(2).
- **Exposure and what lies below.** The south-side step is "great" exposure (1). From the belay, looking back down the ridge, you see a stack of blocks with the tabular block jutting out. To the right (north) is a steep slope with snow patches and conifers in early July. A lake with snowy shores sits up and to the left (2, photo 03). Source 4's photo 27 shows two climbers sitting on a small ledge on the dark flank below a steep buttress with a jutting overhang, with haze on the horizon (4).

### Pitch 3: the Slab (topo P3; first half of P3 in (1), (2) and (4))

- **Length and grade.** The slab itself is short, about 6 m (4) or about 20 ft (2). **5.7, the classic crux** (1)(2)(4)(5).
- **Sequence.**
  1. **Clip the old piton** at the base. It is rusty and "manky" (1, photo 110499191 caption) (2)(4)(5). Source 2 backed it up with a micro-cam (2). Source 4 slung a block above the belay as a backup (4).
  2. **Friction up the slab.** It is a steep, tilted, triangular face of light grey granite flecked with black. Its **left edge is an arête** (4, photos 31-33; 1, photos 106844195 and 110262377).
  3. The feet are good the whole way (2). Several solid footholds sit low, then the right hand pulls around the arête (4). The arête is positive in many places (2). Source 1's photo caption says friction is good, holds are limited, and your feet are your only protection for a few moves (1).
  4. It tops out on a **"glory jug"** (2) or a "nice ledge" (4). The topo's "mercy hold" is this same jug (inference).
  5. Over the lip are rounded blocks, with an overhanging rounded cap boulder to the right (4, photo 33).
- **Crux.** The whole slab. It is short and mentally committing, and above the pin it is run out (1)(2)(4). Source 5 roped up only here, because of the deadly exposure (5).
- **Falls.** The leader is above a single old piton (plus the backup) for about 6 m. A fall from the top would be a long slide or swing onto the pin (inference). The belayer sits right at the foot on the flat platform described above (4, photo 30).
- **Belay (topo version).** The topo ends this pitch only after the traverse and the down-climb, at a "belay from flake". Source 5 instead belayed **immediately above the slab** (5). Sources 1, 2 and 4 run on into the traverse below.
- **Side of the crest.** On the crest. Photos show the slab with drops on both sides (1, photo 116752918 "Exposure on pitch 3"; 1, photo 110262377).
- **Exposure and what lies below.** It is the most exposed spot on the route. Source 1's photo 116752918 shows a climber (blue helmet, red pack) on the slab's edge above a huge drop to a granite basin with a lake. Source 1's photo 110262377 looks straight down on a climber in orange on lichen-black slab. To the side is a steep drop to pale talus with lines of conifers (1). Source 5's photo 0320 shows the north side: a deep talus valley with lines of green conifers (5).

### Pitch 4: the Traverse, the Egg Boulder, Au Cheval (topo P4; second half of P3 in (1), (2) and (4))

- **Length and grade.** Long and wandering, with bad rope drag (2). Easy, but source 5 calls the down-climb mid fifth class (5).
- **Sequence.** The sources give these features in slightly different orders, so the order is partly inference.
  1. Back to the **south side** for more exposure (1). Source 4 went along the **north** side **under a rounded boulder perched on the crest** (4).
  2. The **"famous large boulder"** on top of the ridge. It is a huge egg- or teardrop-shaped boulder balanced on stacked blocks, and climbers **tiptoe round it, hugging it** (2, photo 04; 1, photos 105876096 and 106335910; 4, photo 34).
  3. A **knife-edge fin**. Source 2 says this is an even more exposed block, which you either tiptoe around or pass **au cheval** (straddling it, one leg down each side) (2)(4)(5). Source 5's photos 0332 and 0340 show a climber sitting astride the sharp granite edge with hands on it, and the egg boulder looming just beyond.
  4. **"Tricky slabby down-climb"** on blank slab, just below the egg boulder (5, photo 0337). This is the topo's "down-climb, great exposure" (inference). Source 1 also says to drop onto the north side and down-climb (1).
  5. A **second au cheval** (5, photo 0340). Then the "last cool arête section", a sharp triangular fin with the rope draped over the crest and a lake far below (4, photo 35).
  6. Along a **trail** (1), or mostly walking on a **flat grassy section** (2), to a large ledge system (1)(2)(4). Source 4's photo 36 shows a narrow dirt-and-heather path along the foot of a steep, dark, lichen-covered wall.
- **"Stay north for hidden foot ramp".** This is only on the topo, written as the alternative to the au cheval. No source mentions a hidden ramp. However, source 4 did take the north side under the boulder (4).
- **"Sidewalk".** Only on the topo. Source 1's "trail" and source 2's "flat grassy section" are probably the same ledges (inference).
- **Crux.** The exposure on the knife edge and the slabby down-climb (5). Source 5 says it looks harder than it is (5).
- **Falls.** On a traverse a second could swing as much as a leader could fall. Pieces are sparse: source 4 placed only a #0.4 and a #1 on the slab and the whole traverse (4). The rope over the fin is what would hold them (inference from photo 35).
- **Belay.** A large, nice ledge system (1)(2). It is a **broad ledge** where everything slingable was just out of reach. Source 4 first used a knee-high boulder, which was too low for his belay device to lock off, so he moved a few metres higher to a better boulder (4). Source 2 built the anchor from a slung **horn**, a green cam and a nut, set in coarse speckled granite between rounded pillars with a grass tuft (2, photo 05). Three people fit (2)(4).
- **Side of the crest.** It weaves: south, north, astride the crest, north again for the trail (1)(2)(4)(5).
- **Exposure and what lies below.** Both sides drop away. South: deep basins with lakes (Leprechaun and Viviane are on that side, per the captions in (4) and (5)). North: talus and larch valleys, and the white Enchantments plateau. Behind: snowy jagged peaks (5, photos 0332-0340; 2, photo 04; 1, photo 106335910).

### Pitch 5: Finger Crack or Off-Width, Flake, Chimney, the Hole (topo P5; P4 in (1), (2) and (5); P4 plus a short P5 in (4))

- **Length and grade.** 30-40 ft of very easy ground, or "4th class", to a stance (2)(5). Then the crux crack: **5.8 finger crack** (2) or **5.6 off-width** (2). Source 1 calls the left option off-width with a bulge pulled on hand jams, about 5.7, and the right option a layback crack (1). Source 4 counts the finger crack as the route's second 5.7 crux and found it harder (4).
- **Sequence.**
  1. Scramble up blocky ledges to the stance at the foot of the headwall (5, photos 0346 and 104523; 4, photos 37-38).
  2. **Choice of crack.** Source 5's photo 104523 shows the off-width on the right as a wide crack in a big, clean, pale vertical face.
     - **Finger crack.** A single thin, straight crack up the inside of a shallow **open-book corner**. The left wall is vertical, coarse and speckled. The right side is a smoother, slabby fin, and a **square block juts out at the top**. It is ramp-shaped and turns vertical (2, photo 06; 5, photo 0351; 4, photos 39-40). Source 2 climbed it with the right arête and smeared feet (2).
     - **Off-width with a bulge.** Hand jams, with a .75 cam at chin height (1). Source 5's partner chose it (5).
  3. It tops out on a **lip jug** (2). The topo's "crack with mantle" fits this (inference). There is **one random bolt at the lip** (2).
  4. Easy climbing round a corner to one more ledge (2). Source 4 went up a **flake** to a narrow ledge below the summit off-width (4). This is the topo's "lieback flake, trend right and up to chimney" (inference) and source 1's "layback crack" (inference).
  5. **Two chimneys** guard the summit (2). The topo says to take the left one, through the hole to be birthed to the summit (2, quoting the topo). Source 2 found the hole claustrophobic and topped out on huge jugs on the right side instead (2). Source 5 moved to the south (climber's right) side of a corner and climbed a short, vertical off-width chimney (5). Source 4 calls it short and hard to protect, and says climbers squirm up it (4). Its photos show a tall leaning pillar on the left and a wide slot to another block (4, photos 45-46). Source 5's photo 0367 shows a climber deep in a narrow slot between two lichen-blotched walls, standing on stacked chockstones (5).
- **Crux.** The finger crack (2)(4).
- **Falls.** This is the one real fall in the sources. On the finger crack, after two pieces, a foothold broke under source 2's left foot. **The #0.2 cam five or six feet below held**, like a normal sport fall (2). The leader hung, pulled back on, placed a micro-cam and finished (2). Source 4 protected the crack with a #0.5, #0.3 and #0.75, then a #1 and #3 on the flake, and a #2 low in the summit chimney (4).
- **Belays.** Source 4 used a **horn** on the narrow ledge below the summit off-width (4). Its photo 44 shows a **tiny perch where two people sit squeezed together** with the rope pile. It is a three-person stance only with crowding (inference). Others belayed from the summit itself: a "pinch point" anchor (5), or belaying the second through a notch (1). Source 2's stance on top was comfortable enough to take shoes off (2).
- **Side of the crest.** The headwall is on the crest. The final corner is reached from its south (climber's right) side (5).
- **Exposure and what lies below.** The crack itself is not exposed (5). From its top you look down on talus and golden larches far below (4, photo 42). On the flake, a deep blue lake lies in a forested basin far below (4, photo 43).

## 4. The Summit

- **Size and shape.** Narrow, but there is room to spread out and look over the **vertical south face** (4). It is a jumble of a few large blocks, rounded and squared, with deep slots between them (4, photos 48-51; 5, photos 0378 and 0406). Source 5's photo 0378 shows a climber sitting on the edge of a long, flat-topped summit block, with a deep cleft beside it. The highest point is a rounded boulder blotched with dark lichen. Two people can stand side by side on it (1, photo 112219280; 5, photo 0400).
- **What's on it.** Blocks, lichen, and a little stacked rubble (4, photo 46). I think one photo shows a metal ring or bolt hanger on a summit block (4, photo 51), but I am not certain. Source 2's random bolt is at the finger-crack lip, not on top (2). The first rappel station is on the **north** side of the crest, a short way from the top (4).
- **Three people on it.** Source 2's summit selfie shows the three of them packed together on a rough, speckled block. One wears an orange helmet and an orange hoodie with a radio clipped to the strap. One wears a white helmet, a white sun hoody and a braid. One wears a white helmet, a grey sun hoody and a black pack (2, photo 07). Behind them: a big snow-streaked jagged peak and a turquoise lake with snowy shores (July) (2). For a side-on scene (inference): two standing on the top boulder and one sitting or belaying just below in a slot, all small against open sky, with the south face dropping sheer on one side.
- **Neighbours.** The ridge carries on east to **The Temple**, a jagged crest with a sharp horn summit and steep fluted grey walls (5, photo 0426).

## 6. What It Looks Like, for the Artist

- **Two kinds of rock.**
  - **Lower ridge (pitches 1-4).** Dark grey granite, heavily blotched with black lichen, so it reads as black-and-white "salt and pepper" at a distance. It has lime-green and rust-orange lichen patches, and orange iron staining on some block faces (2, photos 02 and 05; 4, photos 15-26). The shapes are rounded boulders, tilted slab panels and stacked squarish blocks, with grass tufts and dwarf pine in the cracks (2)(4).
  - **Upper tower and south face.** Much paler: cream to very light grey, smoother, with long **vertical crack lines** and pale yellow-green lichen streaks. Flat, squarish cap blocks sit on top (1, photos 106844195 and 110499191; 5, photo 0324). The P1 crack sits in a band of almost **white** rock (2)(5).
- **The ridge in profile from the south.** It rises left (west) to right (east). There is a low, dark, shrubby bump at the far left (the scramble), then a run of jagged gendarmes and stacked boulders: the roof, the slab, the egg boulder, the fins. It ends in a **sharp, pale summit spire**. The spire's **south face** is a sheer wall, fluted with long vertical streaks and grooves (4, photos 13 and 59; 5, photo 0271; 2, photo 10; topo). To the right, the darker, jagged **Temple** ridge continues (5). From the west at dusk there is a smaller second spire just right of the summit (2, photo 10).
- **Background by section.**
  - **Pitches 1-2, looking west and back.** A broad basin of white, glacier-polished slab with larches and small tarns. A snow-flecked peak in the Little Annapurna or Enchantment Peak direction. Lakes far off (Perfection and Inspiration, per captions in (2) and (5)) (4, photos 19-20; 5, photo 0284). A wide slab basin with two lakes in the haze (4, photo 30).
  - **Pitches 3-4.** South: deep basins with blue lakes. North: talus valleys with lines of larch or fir. Snowy jagged ridges on the skyline (1, photos 106335910, 107278335, 116752918 and 124100206; 5, photos 0332-0340).
  - **Summit.** **Lake Viviane with McClellan Peak** behind (5). **Enchantment Peak** (5). **Inspiration and Perfection Lakes and Little Annapurna** (5). **Shield Lake** (5). The lakes below the south side (4). The Temple to the east (5). On a clear day from the hike, Rainier far off (2, photo 08).
- **Seasons in the photos.**
  - **Early July.** Lots of snow on the peaks and patches in the basins, partly frozen lakes, green larches, deep blue sky (2; 1, photos 106335910 and 112219280).
  - **Mid-September.** Smoke haze, overcast, showers, the first yellow larches (5).
  - **Late September.** **Golden larches** everywhere, rolling clouds with sun rays (4). Source 1's photo 124100206 is a striking warm-light shot: orange-gold larches on grey granite, a lake below, a climber topping the slab.
- **Weather and wind.** One trip had calm, hot sun with mosquitoes on the summit, after **high winds** the night before (2). Another had ominous clouds and **strong gusts** over Viviane Lake that came to nothing (4). The third had smoke and unforecast showers (5).
- **Clothing and gear in the photos.**
  - **Helmets.** White is most common, plus orange, blue, teal, red and yellow (1)(2)(4)(5).
  - **Tops.** Sun hoodies in white, grey or bright blue, orange shirts or hoodies, black or purple puffies, purple or blue jackets (2)(4)(5)(1).
  - **Legs.** Black leggings, grey or tan pants, red pants, shorts (1)(4)(5).
  - **Packs.** Small packs: yellow, red, teal, black (1)(4)(5).
  - **Racks.** Big racks of cams and draws on the harness (5, photo 0314). Approach shoes clipped to the back of the harness (4, photo 26).
  - **Ropes.** Bright green, orange, pink-and-white, teal, red (2)(4)(5)(1). Parties of three often trail two ropes (2)(4).
- **Wildlife.** Mountain goats play on a snowfield near camp below Isolation Lake (2, text only). One shaggy white goat on granite under trees near Inspiration Lake (5, photo 0238). A fat marmot near Perfection Lake (5, caption). No birds or goats appear on the route itself in any photo I saw.

## 7. How Parties of Three Climb It

- **Source 2 (three people).** One leader with two followers on two ropes, a 70 m and a 60 m (2). The followers climbed together: source 2's photo 04 shows the two of them tiptoeing round the boulder side by side. Their pitch 3 photo shows two rope strands (2). They were slow as a three (2).
- **Source 4 (three people).** A three-person system on a doubled 60 m twin rope, with the lead rotating each pitch. Followers came up one at a time (4). The doubled 60 m was short on the traverse, so the author recommends a 70 m for three (4).
- **Source 5 (a pair).** Soloed most of it, and roped only the slab, the finger crack and the summit chimney. Not a party of three (5).

## 8. Disagreements and Gaps

- **Pitch count.** 4 (1)(2)(5), 5 (topo), effectively 5 (4). The difference comes from splitting the slab-and-traverse pitch (topo) and the summit off-width (4).
- **Rappel count.** 3 (2), 4 (1)(4), 5 (5)(topo). Doubled ropes explain part of it (2).
- **Last pitch.** Source 1 says off-width on the left and layback crack on the right. Source 2 has a 5.6 off-width and a 5.8 finger crack. Source 4 has a finger crack (5.7, felt harder) and an off-width. The topo has crack and mantle, lieback flake, then the left chimney. I can't say for sure which crack is the topo's "crack with mantle" (inference: the finger crack, finished on the lip jug).
- **Order of the egg boulder and the au chevals.** Source 2 has the boulder, then the au cheval block. Source 4 has the north side under the boulder, then the knife edge. Source 5's photos show an au cheval with the boulder ahead, a down-climb below it, then another au cheval.
- **Which side the traverse uses.** Source 1 says south, then north. Source 4 went north under the boulder. The topo offers au cheval or staying north.
- **Grades.** The slab is 5.7 everywhere. The finger crack is 5.8 (2) or "5.7" and felt harder (4).
- **Not confirmed by any source.** The balanced rock, the start tree, "mercy hold", "sidewalk", the "hidden foot ramp", the "belay from flake" at the end of topo P3, and the "rope eating flake" by name. They exist only on the topo. Each plausible match is marked as an inference above.
- **Missing material.** SummitPost (3) is entirely missing (403). Source 1's 27 comments and its other 25 photos are missing.
